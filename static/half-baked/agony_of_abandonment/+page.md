## The Worst Thing I've Ever Done to Someone

I moved to New Jersey when I was 18 from Austin, Texas. In doing so, I abandoned
one of my best friends, a person among those whome I love most in the world. It
wasn't so much physical distance that constituted abandonment, rather the
emotional and communicative detachement that followed. I still loved him at the
time as I still love him now. I believe he still loves me. I do not believe I am
forgiven, nor do I expect to I be.

As a new chapter of life begins, inevitably people leave. Excusing cases of
protection, only possession would chicane to prevent this, and possession is not
love. My transgression, however, emerges from the fact that I knew he needed me.
I don't remember what I was thinking or if I had a good excuse. I was likely
overwhelmed in a new environment and new place. Doubtfully I was too overwhelmed
to be a good friend though. I do remember how I felt when I came to my senses,
when I became aware of the hurt I had inflicted. This was some years ago now, I
still feel terrible. I will endeavor not do that again.

## Dignity in Melancholy

**Warning: Major spoilers for the Earthsea series ahead**

Recently, I've been attempting to complete (Ursula K. Le
Guin's)[https://www.ursulakleguin.com/] bibliography. A common happenstance is a
an immediate and deep sense of melancholy, in both the depressive and
contemplative sense, settling on me the moment after the final word. No author
has so consistently drawn tears from me. Most recently, I finished the sixth and
final book in the Earthsea series: _The Other Wind_. This series, more so than
her other work that I've read, clearly began for a younger audience. In fact she
states as much in the afterword present in my copy of _A Wizard of Earthsea_. On
top of this, it was narrative fantasy (the only book of Tolkien's Legendarium I
was ever able to make it through was _The Silmarillion_, which I fell in love
with). Frankly I did not enjoy _Earthsea_ so much to begin with. Coming hot off
some of her most critically acclaimed work - that all happened to be part of a
rich cannon of socially transversive science fiction - it felt a stark departure
from the writing I had become obsessed with.

Book one, _A Wizard of Earthsea_, it's set from the perspective of a young man
named Ged, or "Sparrowhawk" as he is primarily known. The book is largely
action, largely adventure. It's not so morally simple as some "young adult"
(read: child) fiction, especially other well-known series concerning a young
wizard, but also given the _Hunger Games_ style of fiction that many young
readers of my generation were drowned in, myself included, it wasn't
particularly thematically complex.

By the time I got to the fifth and final novel of the series _The Other Wind_, I
had already learned how sorely mistaken about the series I was. Granted the
series was written over 30 years, and had evolved with the author. Our young
wizard, Ged, was now a 70 year old man of no magical power. The burden of
adventure was no longer his, but rather the people that he loved and supported.
He had moved the world, but now he was nothing more than a simple man, tending
his small collection of livestock, shunned by the locals who knew him only as an
uncanny man with a disfigured daughter.

This book focuses primarily on the party of heroes who are saving the world as
any good fantasy adventure novel should, but also spends a fair amount of time
on Ged, at his home, worried for his wife and daughter, doing his daily chores:
feeding the goats and the chickens, repairing the fence; Doing what needs to be
done. The following passage is upon the return of Tenar, his wife, after she had
just played a central role in saving the mortal souls of all people (and
dragons):

> "Tell me" she said, "tell me what you did while I was gone."
>
> "Kept the house."
>
> "Did you walk in the forest?"
>
> "Not yet," he said.

_-"The Other Wind", Ursula K. Le Guin_

And the book, the story of Earthsea, ends.

Throughout this series, and throughout much of her work, she makes a case for
the importance of the normal person, the average life. As noble as the selfless
sacrifice and the saving of the world is the daily responsibility of doing what
must be done. In fact they are no different. Ged was never great because of his
power, but his magnanimity was the source of his greatness.

## All this is to say

I'm not really sure where I was going with this. It's been two months since I
wrote the last paragraph and I've thoroughly disembarked from that train of
thought. I think I want to learn from my mistakes. I need to isolate what it is
to be a responsible man, a good person, and a good friend. I've spent so much
time worried about myself, and not enough time worried about my friends.
